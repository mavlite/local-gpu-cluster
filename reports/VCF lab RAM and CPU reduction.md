# Shrink the services runtime before anything else

The biggest safe reduction available to this lab is in the VCF Services Runtime (VSP), not in the classic appliances. Six of the lab's VMs are the 9.1.1 **Small (High Availability)** VSP profile: three 4 vCPU / 10 GB control-plane nodes plus three 10 vCPU / 16 GB workers, **42 vCPU / 78 GB**. Converting it to Simple (one control plane) has been done in a 9.1.1 lab and frees **8 vCPU / 20 GB**. Dropping to two workers would free another **10 vCPU / 16 GB**, but nobody has published that on 9.1.1. After those two, three appliance edits to standard smaller shapes follow: NSX Manager to the Small form factor (−2 vCPU / −8 GB, using KB 403604 mechanics), vCenter to the Tiny shape (−2 vCPU / −7 GB, unsupported downsize), and the Operations collector rebuilt as a classic Cloud Proxy Small (−2 vCPU / −8 GB, documented path, compatibility unverified). Together these take the VCF footprint from about **64 vCPU / 167 GB configured to about 40 vCPU / 104–108 GB**.

The lab's measured CPU problem is different. NSX Manager drew **8.96 GHz**, two-thirds of all non-VSP appliance CPU. That matches a known AMD Zen entropy issue with a low-risk fix, and it should be the first change. Three things should stay as they are: VCF Operations (already at its 2/8 floor), the License Server, and the collector's existence. The point of shrinking is less the headline gigabytes than what they allow afterwards: once the critical control-plane VMs are small, they can be kept in DRAM with a full reservation and tiering turned off. That takes them off the consumer NVMe tier that has crashed VMs here before. Every number below comes from a snapshot taken minutes after a cold start, so each cut must wait for a 7-day measurement window.

## The lab is already lean, so only structure changes are left

The lab's starting point is unusually lean, so there is little easy slack to remove. Adding up the supplied figures gives **22 vCPU / 89 GB** across the six classic appliances and **42 vCPU / 78 GB** for VSP. Active memory right after boot was roughly **32 GB for the appliances and 24–37 GB for VSP**. Every comparable published build runs on twice this lab's DRAM per host. Most use Minisforum MS-A2 hosts with 128 GB each. Daniel Krieger's full 9.0.1 build totalled **76 vCPU / 314 GB** on two of them ([SDN-Warrior](https://sdn-warrior.org/posts/vcf9-ms-a2-special/)). William Lam's 9.0 minimum was **194 GB including VCF Automation** ([William Lam](https://williamlam.com/2025/06/minimal-resources-for-deploying-vcf-9-0-in-a-lab.html)). The only published attempt on this class of hardware, three 8-core 64 GB NUCs, cut a VSP worker to 8 vCPU and ended with pods stalled on Telemetry ([William Lam, 9.1 workarounds comments](https://williamlam.com/2026/05/vcf-9-1-comprehensive-vcf-installer-sddc-manager-configuration-workarounds-for-lab-deployments.html)). Three 64 GB hosts is not a configuration anyone has documented as comfortable.

The size options in the documentation confirm that little slack remains. VCF Operations is already at **Extra Small (2 vCPU / 8 GB)**, the bottom of Broadcom's sizing table ([KB 397782](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/397782/)). SDDC Manager has a single size of 4/16 ([William Lam](https://williamlam.com/2025/06/minimal-resources-for-deploying-vcf-9-0-in-a-lab.html)). The License Server at 2/4 matches the only published figure for it ([Leaha](https://blog.leaha.co.uk/2026/05/06/vcf-9-1-x-ultimate-deployment-guide/)). VSP workers are already the right-sized 9.1.1 `management.small` machine type (10 vCPU / 16 GB), and the lab's 42/78 total equals the wizard's Small HA quote exactly ([virtualbytes.io](https://virtualbytes.io/vcf-9-1-1-scaling-vcf-management-services-from-ha-back-to-simple/)). The remaining savings therefore come from changing the topology (HA to Simple, fewer workers) and from moving three appliances down to the next standard shape. Tuning inside the guest is not where the savings are.

This lab differs from the community builds in where the risk sits. Krieger measured almost no Tier 1 use for his management domain on 128 GB hosts ([SDN-Warrior](https://sdn-warrior.org/posts/vcf9-ms-a2-special/)). Here, hosts report **72 / 64 / 81 GB used against 64 GB of DRAM each**, so two hosts already exceed DRAM. Tiering also engages earlier than that: at about **80% DRAM consumption, roughly 51 GB per host**, after which cold pages start moving to NVMe ([KB 449016](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/449016/understanding-nvme-memory-tiering-activa.html)). After a tier NVMe failure the host stays up, but VMs whose cold pages were on that device can eventually fail ([VMware blog](https://blogs.vmware.com/cloud-foundation/2025/11/18/nvme-memory-tiering-design-and-sizing-on-vmware-cloud-foundation-9-part-2/)), which matches this lab's vMotion crashes. For context, though it is outside this report's scope: devvm01-03 alone are configured at **144 GB**, more than the whole VCF stack after the proposed cuts. Any change to them will shift tier use more than any single VCF change can.

An inference shapes how to read every saving below. These VMs have no reservations, and ESXi keeps backing every page a guest has touched until it is under pressure, and on these hosts pressure means the NVMe tier. Java-heavy appliances such as NSX, SDDC Manager and Operations grow toward their configured size over days. The configured size is therefore the real ceiling on what each VM will eventually consume. The active figure measured at boot is a floor, not the steady state. That is why configured-size cuts are worth making even though active memory looks small today.

## Proposals ranked by savings against risk

| # | Change | vCPU saved | GB saved (configured) | Support status | Risk | Revert before VCF upgrade? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Measure for 7 days (prerequisite) | — | — | n/a | none | n/a |
| 1 | Fix NSX Manager entropy CPU burn on Zen | 0 (CPU MHz only, est. several GHz) | 0 | Unsupported lab tweak, documented by Lam/Fojta | Low | No (re-check after upgrade) |
| 2 | VSP Small HA → Simple (`cluster.ha=false`) | 8 | 20 | Unsupported conversion to a supported shape | Medium | No; verify health first |
| 3 | VSP workers 3 → 2 | 10 | 16 | Unsupported, unproven on 9.1.1 | Medium-high | Yes |
| 4 | NSX Manager Medium 6/24 → Small 4/16 | 2 | 8 | KB 403604 mechanics; Small is lab-only | Medium | Yes |
| 5 | vCenter Small 4/21 → Tiny shape 2/14 | 2 | 7 | Downsize explicitly unsupported | Medium | Yes |
| 6 | Ops collector UCP Small 4/16 → Cloud Proxy Small 2/8 (new collector group) | 2 | 8 | Documented migration path; CP-vs-UCP fit unverified | Medium | Keep only if upgrade precheck passes |
| 7 | Pin vcsa, nsxa, licsrv in DRAM (tiering off + full reservation) | 0 | 0 (reduces tier exposure by ~34 GB after #4-5) | Supported settings | Low-medium | No |
| 8 | SDDC Manager 16 → 12 GB, or part-time power-off | 0 | 4 (or 16 while off) | Untested | Medium | Yes (power on, restore) |
| — | Leave alone: ops 2/8, licsrv 2/4, collector existence, `Mem.TierNVMePct` | — | — | — | — | — |

If #1–#6 all hold, the VCF stack goes to roughly **40 vCPU / 108 GB configured**, or 104 GB with #8. The active-memory saving is smaller. Removing three VSP nodes takes out about **12–16 GB of active memory**. The appliance edits mainly lower how much each VM can eventually consume. That is still enough to bring the VCF stack's likely steady-state consumption comfortably under the cluster's **~154 GB 80%-of-DRAM trigger** with room to spare. Whether hosts actually stay below the trigger depends on the out-of-scope devvms.

The ordering reflects three judgments. First, the VSP changes come before the appliance edits because they are larger and because the shape they land on is a supported one, even though the route to it is not. Second, CPU is not the cluster's constraint. About **28–30 GHz of 119.7 GHz** was in use at boot, and vCPU overcommit is under 2:1 per core with the benchmark and runner VMs off, well below the 2.4–4.75:1 where Krieger hit trouble ([SDN-Warrior](https://sdn-warrior.org/posts/vcf9-ms-a2-special/)). So pure vCPU trims rank low, except NSX, whose burn is anomalous. Third, the measurement step comes first because the supplied snapshot shows boot behaviour, not steady state.

## Measure for a week, then cut one change at a time

**Step 0, the measurement window.** Broadcom's rightsizing guidance sets 24 hours as the minimum collection period. Its thresholds are: CPU ready below about 5% per vCPU, co-stop below about 3% per vCPU, and balloon and host swap at a sustained zero ([KB 438023](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/438023/rightsizing-virtual-machines-on-esxi-80.html)). That is too short for VCF appliances, whose peaks come from periodic jobs. Run for **7 days**, and make sure the window includes one backup cycle, one SDDC Manager bundle download or LCM precheck, and one certificate or password-expiry check. Record these per VM: Memory Active, Consumed, Balloon, Swapped and the in-guest used figure. Add per-VM Tier 1 consumption from `memstats -r vmtier-stats -u mb -s name:memSize:active:tier1Target:tier1Consumed` ([KB 449016](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/449016/understanding-nvme-memory-tiering-activa.html)). Record CPU Ready % and Co-stop %. Per host, record DRAM consumed against the 80% line. For VSP, add `kubectl top nodes` and a check for Pending pods. Size each cut against the **maximum** active value seen over the week plus margin, not the average. After every individual change, run a fresh **48–72 hour** soak covering at least one nightly job before making the next one. One change at a time keeps each regression attributable.

**#1, NSX entropy (do first).** Consumer AMD Zen 4/5 CPUs drive NSX Manager CPU high through slow entropy. The published fixes are an entropy setting on the VM and the ESXi `entropySources` host setting. Fojta used `entropySources=4` to cure NSX transport nodes going UNKNOWN ([William Lam, ESX workarounds](https://williamlam.com/2026/05/vcf-9-1-comprehensive-esx-configuration-workarounds-for-lab-deployments.html); [Fojta](https://fojta.wordpress.com/2025/06/19/deploying-vcf-9-in-a-minilab/)). To measure, first confirm that nsxa stays above about 5 GHz after the first hour, because 8.96 GHz may be partly boot load. Then compare NSX CPU MHz and guest `get processes monitor` output before and after across a 24-hour period. The rollback is to remove the VM or host setting and reboot. These are host and VM tweaks that VCF does not manage, so they need not be reverted before an upgrade, but re-check them after any ESX upgrade.

**#2, VSP HA to Simple.** First run `kubectl get components -A`. If `ops-logs` or Real-time Metrics shows up, removing it comes first, because Log Management pushes workers to `management.large` (16/32) and Real-time Metrics adds a fourth worker ([virtualbytes.io](https://virtualbytes.io/vcf-9-1-1-worker-rightsizing-poddisruptionbudgets-and-why-it-does-not-stick/)). The 3-worker Small HA footprint suggests neither is installed. The proven 9.1.1 procedure is `vmsp pkg configure vmsp-platform -n vmsp-platform --set cluster.ha=false`, run as root on a control-plane node with `KUBECONFIG=/etc/kubernetes/admin.conf`. It takes about 20 minutes. Expect these follow-ups:

- The fleet-LCM, SDDC-LCM and salt-raas Postgres instances get stuck in SyncFailed and need a `sync-nudge` annotation each.
- The Kyverno webhook is briefly unreachable.
- VCF Operations needs `kubectl rollout restart deploy -n vcf-fleet-lcm` before it stops showing HA.
- Orphaned `pgdata-*-db-2` PVCs are left behind.

All of this is reported by [virtualbytes.io](https://virtualbytes.io/vcf-9-1-1-scaling-vcf-management-services-from-ha-back-to-simple/). Take a **VCFMS backup** beforehand. VM snapshots are not a valid rollback, because every control-plane VM is rebuilt. Use the safe shutdown script for any later power cycle ([KB 440874](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/440874/)). The rollback is the supported 9.1.1 Day-N action that scales Small to Small HA in VCF Operations ([William Lam](https://williamlam.com/2026/09/10-exciting-enhancements-in-vmware-cloud-foundation-9-1-1.html)). Leave Simple in place for upgrades, since it is a shape Broadcom deploys. Before upgrading, confirm all Postgres instances are healthy and that Operations shows the correct topology. Lam reports that HA for Simple is on the roadmap ([William Lam](https://williamlam.com/2026/06/vcf-9-1-enabling-high-availability-for-a-small-vcf-management-services-vcfms-deployment.html)). The cost of Simple is that a host failure takes VSP down until vSphere HA restarts its single control plane. That is acceptable for a single-operator lab.

**#3, VSP workers to two.** The lever is `cluster.worker.minReplicas`, and it sets a floor, not a fixed count: the autoscaler moved a cluster from 3 to 4 workers and back during a rollout ([virtualbytes.io](https://virtualbytes.io/vcf-9-1-1-worker-rightsizing-poddisruptionbudgets-and-why-it-does-not-stick/)). Lam says 9.1.1 Simple deployments have "one less" worker than 9.1.0's three ([William Lam](https://williamlam.com/2026/09/vcf-9-1-1-adopting-the-reduced-vcf-management-services-vcfms-footprint-after-an-upgrade.html)). That suggests two workers is the greenfield Simple shape, but this lab is the fleet instance, and KB 455842's script picks a higher minimum for fleet deployments ([KB 455842](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/455842)). Before setting anything by hand, read the script's case statement for the Simple-plus-fleet value. If it says 2, running the supported script after #2 is the cleaner route. If it says 3, two workers is off-script and may need Leaha-style trimming of pod requests to fit. Leaha calls that "NOT even remotely supported" ([Leaha](https://blog.leaha.co.uk/2026/07/21/vcf-9-management-services-lab-downsize/)). Each worker roll stalls on single-replica Postgres PodDisruptionBudgets for up to 1,800 seconds. Deleting the blocking pod clears the stall ([virtualbytes.io](https://virtualbytes.io/vcf-9-1-1-worker-rightsizing-poddisruptionbudgets-and-why-it-does-not-stick/)). To measure, watch for Pending pods and autoscaler scale-ups over 72 hours. The rollback is to set `minReplicas=3`. Do that before any upgrade, because a patch may recompute the profile anyway.

**#4, NSX Manager to Small.** KB 403604 describes the procedure: wait for a stable cluster, shut the VM down in vSphere, edit it to a standard form factor, power it on, and wait for STABLE. SDDC Manager needs no update for Small, Medium or Large. The KB is written for three-node clusters, does not address downsizing, and marks Small as lab and proof-of-concept only ([KB 403604](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/403604/changing-the-form-factor-of-nsx-manager.html)). Use exactly **4 vCPU / 16 GB**. A non-standard shape such as 4/12 does not match any defined profile. Nobody has published NSX at Small on VCF 9, and the measured **13.9 GB active at 24 GB** makes 16 GB tight. Do #1 first, so the measurement is not confused by the entropy CPU burn. Then watch for the "memory usage high" and "very high" alarms ([KB 375204](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/375204/manager-memory-usage-high-alarm.html), [KB 375215](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/375215/nsx-manager-memory-usage-very-high-alarm.html)) and for guest swap over 7 days. Abandon the change if NSX active memory stays above about 14 GB. The rollback is the same power-off edit back to 6/24. Revert before any NSX upgrade, because no source says whether 9.1.x prechecks flag Small.

**#5, vCenter to the Tiny shape.** vCenter 9.1 still defines Tiny at **2 vCPU / 14 GB** for up to 10 hosts ([Broadcom TechDocs](https://techdocs.broadcom.com/us/en/vmware-cis/vsphere/vsphere/9-1/vcenter-installation-and-setup/deploying-the-vcenter-server-appliance/vcenter-server-appliance-requirements/vcenter-server-appliance-hardware-requirements.html)). Lam's VCF 9.0 installer JSON deploys it ([William Lam](https://williamlam.com/2025/06/ultimate-lab-resource-for-vcf-9-0.html)). However, Broadcom states that "downsizing is not supported" for an existing vCenter ([VMware blog](https://blogs.vmware.com/cloud-foundation/2026/05/12/resizing-vmware-vcenter/)), so the only way to get there is a manual power-off edit. With 4.2 GB active and 1.4 GHz in use, vCenter has the most headroom of any appliance. To measure, watch guest memory, vSphere Client responsiveness and boot time across a full cold start of the stack. A cold start is the peak for vCenter, and Krieger saw vCenter time out under CPU contention ([SDN-Warrior](https://sdn-warrior.org/posts/vcf9-ms-a2-special/)). The rollback is the supported `PATCH deployment/size` resize back to Small, or a power-off edit. The API resize will not run while vCenter has snapshots ([VMware blog](https://blogs.vmware.com/cloud-foundation/2026/05/12/resizing-vmware-vcenter/)), so delete them first. Revert before upgrades, because no source covers what the reduced-downtime upgrade does when the VM is smaller than its recorded size.

**#6, the Operations collector.** The collector itself must stay. A local collector on the Ops node fails 9.1 registration validation ([KB 443106](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/443106/vcf-instance-cloud-proxy-registration-va.html)), and without a cloud proxy in the instance the 9.1 upgrade cannot complete ([WWT](https://www.wwt.com/blog/before-you-upgrade-to-vcf-91-what-you-need-to-have-ready)). Broadcom calls resizing a deployed cloud proxy in place "deprecated". The supported route is a new collector group at the new size, moving the adapters to it, and then deleting the old group ([KB 457248](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/457248/scaling-cloud-proxy-nodes-in-vcf-operati.html)). Classic Cloud Proxy Small is 2/8, against 4/16 for the Unified Cloud Proxy Small the lab runs ([KB 397782](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/397782/)). Nobody has confirmed that a classic proxy serves every 9.1 fleet integration. Keep the old group powered off but intact for 7 days, and during that time check adapter collection status, SDDC Manager and VCFMS integration health in Ops, and the KB 443106 validation. The rollback is to move the adapters back to the old group. An easier fallback, also supported, cuts vCPU only (4 to 2, RAM unchanged): KB 397782 permits vCPU reduction on lightly loaded clusters below 60% CPU.

**#7, pinning the control plane in DRAM.** The per-VM switch is `sched.mem.enableTiering = FALSE`. Broadcom calls an unreserved tier-disabled VM "risky" and says it should be fully reserved. A reservation by itself does **not** keep pages in DRAM ([VMware blog, Aug 2026](https://blogs.vmware.com/cloud-foundation/2026/08/06/memory-tiering-and-vm-memory-reservation-what-you-actually-need-to-know/)). After #4 and #5, pinning vcsa (14), nsxa (16) and licsrv (4) costs **34 GB of reserved DRAM**, against 49 GB at today's sizes. That difference is why the shrinks come first. Place the three VMs on different hosts. Do not reserve VSP nodes: CAPI rebuilds those VMs, and whether vCenter-side settings survive a rollout is untested. To measure, `memstats` should show zero `tier1Consumed` for the pinned VMs, and host DRAM should stay under 80% with the reservations in place. The rollback is to remove the setting and the reservation. Do not raise `Mem.TierNVMePct` above its default of 100 ([VMware blog](https://blogs.vmware.com/cloud-foundation/?p=28715)), because a larger ratio puts more pages on the drives that have hung. Skip the experiment of relaxing TPS salting: it reopens a side channel for little gain ([VMware KB 2097593](https://kb.vmware.com/kb/2097593)).

**#8, SDDC Manager.** Nobody reports SDDC Manager running below 16 GB on 9.x. A cut to 12 GB is a guess, and the risk is out-of-memory failures during bundle downloads and prechecks, which are exactly the moments it is needed. Powering it off between maintenance windows saves all 4/16 while it is off. The cost is that VCF Operations loses its SDDC data feed ([KB 440732](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/440732/vcf-instance-inventory-or-objects-such-a.html)), and scheduled password and certificate work does not run. Treat it as optional and last. Power it on and let it settle for a day before any upgrade.

## Keep licensing and Operations untouched; they fail closed

Broadcom says to keep the License Server VM **powered on at all times**. Licensed vCenters keep their entitlements while it or Operations is down, but assignments and capacity changes are blocked ([KB 443240](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/443240)). Operations is the ongoing licensing authority and cannot be decommissioned ([KB 446056](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/446056/licensing-vcf-operations-91-in-disconnec.html)). Usage must be reported at least every **180 days**, or hosts disconnect ([KB 438771](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/438771/getting-error-usage-report-overdue-today.html)). Shrinking licsrv from 2/4 saves almost nothing, and this lab's license fails closed, so licsrv, ops and the collector stay powered on. One open question matters for future power-off schedules: KB 440471 describes the License Server as deployed "within VCF Management Services" ([KB 440471](https://knowledge.broadcom.com/external/article/440471/one-or-more-vcenter-instances-are-not-co.html)), so a VSP shutdown may interrupt licensing even with the licsrv VM still on. Never schedule VSP downtime near the "Next Usage Report Due" date.

Before any VCF upgrade, use this checklist:

- Restore NSX to Medium (#4) and vCenter to Small (#5), after deleting vCenter snapshots.
- Set VSP `minReplicas` back to 3 (#3).
- Power on SDDC Manager and let it settle (#8).
- Confirm the collector group passes cloud-proxy validation (#6). If it does not, move the adapters back to a UCP.
- Check that the Simple VSP's Postgres instances are all healthy (#2).

Changes #1, #2 and #7 can stay in place through an upgrade.

## Conclusion

Most of the remaining savings are in VSP topology, not in appliance tuning. That inverts the usual homelab advice, which comes from 9.0, where VCF Automation was the 96 GB problem. On 9.1.1 without Automation, the six VSP VMs make up nearly half of configured VCF memory, and the most valuable change is a route that has been proven in a lab to a shape Broadcom supports. The appliance edits matter for a reason the community write-ups do not face, because they run on 128 GB hosts. Here, shrinking vCenter and NSX is what makes it affordable to reserve them in DRAM, and that matters more for stability than the gigabytes saved.

Two things remain unknown. Nobody has published whether a downsized NSX, vCenter or VSP survives a 9.1.x patch upgrade. Nobody has published whether this lab's Simple-plus-fleet profile accepts two workers. Treat the first VCF upgrade after these changes as a test. Revert as the checklist says, take notes, and publish the result, because that data point does not exist yet.
